# Methodology: seed and seedling microbiome meta-analysis

The study was designed as a meta-analysis of published 16S rRNA amplicon datasets, following Simonin *et al.* (2022), to examine how bacterial communities change across the seed-to-seedling transition. An ASV count table, a taxonomy table, and harmonised sample metadata keyed on run accession were merged into a single phyloseq object; the compiled data span eight studies, 16 host species, and 10 countries. Developmental stage (i.e., seeds versus seedlings) was the primary contrast, with study, host genus, and plant family as secondary variables; country of origin was recorded as a description of the dataset and not tested. Study was treated as a confounder throughout rather than as one more factor; this, in part, is because the dataset is heavily unbalanced, with a single study contributing most of the samples.

Filtering was applied in a fixed order. Only ASVs assigned to the kingdom Bacteria were retained, and chloroplast (order), mitochondrial (family), and cyanobacterial (phylum) sequences were removed. Soil samples were then excluded, as were samples with fewer than 500 reads; ASVs present in fewer than three samples, or with fewer than 10 reads in total, were removed last. No contaminant removal was applied, as the prevalence method in `decontam` depends on negative controls and the source studies did not deposit any; this is a limitation of working with public data rather than of the pipeline itself.

Alpha diversity was calculated on data rarefied to 500 reads per sample. The filtered count table was randomly subsampled without replacement to an even depth of 500 reads (`vegan::rrarefy`, seed 123), and samples below this depth were excluded. Three indices were then computed per sample: Observed richness (the number of ASVs detected), Shannon diversity (`vegan::diversity`), and Pielou's evenness (Shannon divided by the natural logarithm of richness). Differences between groups were tested with Kruskal–Wallis tests, with effect size expressed as (H − k + 1)/(n − k), where H is the test statistic, k the number of groups, and n the number of samples. While a pooled test is informative, it is dominated by the largest study; the stage comparison was therefore repeated within each study that sampled both stages. Rarefaction to 1,000 reads served as a sensitivity check on the depth chosen.

Beta diversity was analysed on every feature that passed filtering. Counts were normalised by cumulative sum scaling (CSS; metagenomeSeq), with the scaling quantile chosen from the data, as sequencing depth varies widely amidst these studies and a few dominant features should not set the scaling. The ASV level was the primary unit of analysis, with the genus level serving as a sensitivity analysis and as the basis of the ordination figures; ASVs without a genus assignment were dropped on agglomeration, and samples with fewer than two non-zero units were removed. Bray–Curtis dissimilarities were calculated on the CSS-normalised counts (`vegan::vegdist`) and visualised by principal coordinates analysis (PCoA). Group structure was tested by PERMANOVA (`vegan::adonis2`, 999 permutations, seed 123), first for each variable alone and then in a marginal model in which stage, host genus, and study were tested together. Plant family and author were left out of the marginal model, as they are collinear with host genus and study.

Taxonomic composition was summarised at the phylum, family, and genus ranks as the mean of per-sample relative abundances, so that each sample carries equal weight regardless of sequencing depth. Composition is shown per study and stage, as well as per host genus and plant family, because a pooled summary would mostly describe the largest study.

Associations between individual taxa and developmental stage were examined in five ways: (i) indicator analysis was performed on relative abundances (`indicspecies::multipatt`, 999 permutations) using both the IndVal.g statistic and the group-equalised point-biserial correlation coefficient (`r.g`), with significance set at p < 0.05 without correction for multiple testing; (ii) differential abundance was tested with DESeq2, using `poscounts` size factors and stage as the only design term, with significance set at an adjusted p-value below 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change above 1; (iii) the five most abundant genera were correlated with stage by Spearman's method within each host genus and plant family, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction; (iv) co-occurrence networks were built separately for seeds and for seedlings, from pairwise Spearman correlations among the 50 most abundant ASVs of each stage, keeping edges with an absolute ρ of at least 0.6 and a Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-value below 0.05; and (v) the stage effect was estimated within each plant family and within each host genus, and pooled across them by random-effects meta-analysis.

For the stratified analysis (v), the genera considered were those holding at least one ASV significant in both the DESeq2 analysis and the IndVal.g indicator analysis (171 ASVs in 23 genera). Genus counts were transformed to centred log-ratios within each sample after adding a pseudocount of 0.5. The analysis was run twice, with plant family and with host genus (both derived from host species) as the stratifying variable; strata holding at least three samples at each stage were used (six plant families and eight host genera). Within each stratum, the difference between seedlings and seeds was expressed as Hedges' g (`metafor::escalc`, standardised mean difference), a stratum contributing to a genus only where that genus was detected in at least 10% of its samples. For the genera estimated in at least two strata (18 by plant family and 18 by host genus), the per-stratum effects were pooled by random-effects meta-analysis fitted by restricted maximum likelihood: plant families were pooled with the Knapp–Hartung adjustment (`metafor::rma`), and host genera with a random effect of study above that of host genus (`metafor::rma.mv`, t-based test), except for the genera for which no study supplied more than one host genus, which were pooled as for plant families. Pooled p-values were corrected by the Benjamini–Hochberg method across genera, heterogeneity was summarised as I², and the number of strata agreeing in sign with the pooled estimate was recorded for each genus. As a sensitivity analysis, each pooled estimate was refitted with every stratum omitted in turn (leave-one-out), for the genera estimated in at least three strata (10 by plant family and 17 by host genus), and the range of the refitted estimates, the stability of their sign, and the number of refits whose 95% confidence interval excluded zero were recorded.

The results were presented as a two-panel heatmap (plant family and host genus) of Hedges' g, showing the 10 genera estimated in at least three plant families and at least three host genera, each with its pooled estimate.

To test whether host evolutionary relatedness shapes alpha diversity, a time-calibrated phylogeny of the 16 host plant species was obtained from TimeTree 5 (Kumar *et al.*, 2022) and reduced to one tip per species, as subspecies and varieties are returned as separate tips. For each developmental stage and each alpha index (observed richness, Shannon diversity, and Pielou's evenness, all on data rarefied to 500 reads), the mean across samples was taken per host species; host species with fewer than three samples at a stage were excluded from the test for that stage. Phylogenetic signal was quantified with Blomberg's *K* (Blomberg *et al.*, 2003), tested by 999 random permutations of species across the tips, and with Pagel's λ (Pagel, 1999), fitted by maximum likelihood (`phytools::phylosig`). Because species means rest on very unequal numbers of samples, samples were resampled within each species with replacement (B = 500, seed 123), λ was re-estimated at each iteration, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles were reported as a bootstrap 95% confidence interval.

Phylosymbiosis was assessed on the CSS-normalised Bray–Curtis dissimilarities described above. Within each developmental stage, sample-level dissimilarities were averaged over all sample pairs between each pair of host species, and host genetic distance was taken as the divergence time between the two hosts on the TimeTree phylogeny (i.e., half the path length between their tips); host species with fewer than three samples at a stage were excluded. The association between host relatedness and microbiome dissimilarity was tested with Mantel tests (`vegan::mantel`, Pearson and Spearman, 999 permutations) and with multiple regression on distance matrices (MRM; Lichstein, 2007), implemented as ordinary least squares on the lower triangles with significance obtained by permuting the rows and columns of the response matrix together (999 permutations). As most host species were sequenced by a single study, a second MRM included the share of sample pairs that lie between two different studies, so that a host effect could be separated, albeit imperfectly, from a study effect. The genus level was treated as the primary unit, since most ASVs occur in a single study and ASV-level dissimilarities between studies approach their maximum irrespective of the host; the ASV level is reported as a sensitivity analysis. The contributions of host identity and developmental stage were then quantified on the sample-level dissimilarities by marginal PERMANOVA (`vegan::adonis2`, `by = "margin"`, 999 permutations) and by variance partitioning (`vegan::varpart`, adjusted R²), with host and developmental stage as predictors; host family and host genus are nested, so that the marginal effect of one given the other does not exist, and they were therefore fitted in separate models.

Ecological generalists and specialists were identified in two respects, namely developmental stage and host breadth. With respect to stage, genera were classified by the multinomial species classification method (CLAM; Chazdon *et al.*, 2011; `vegan::clamtest`, specialisation threshold 2/3, α = 0.005, coverage limit 10) as seed specialists, seedling specialists, generalists, or too rare to classify; the test was run on the pooled samples of the five studies that sampled both stages, as a genus found only in a study that sampled seedlings would otherwise be classed as a seedling specialist by construction, and was repeated within each of those studies, the number of studies returning the pooled class being recorded for each genus. With respect to host breadth, Levins' niche breadth (B = 1/Σp²) was calculated for each genus across the 16 host species, each host being first reduced to one average sample of 1,000 reads (mean relative abundance multiplied by 1,000 and rounded) so that sample number does not determine breadth; observed breadth was compared with 999 null tables that preserve row sums, column sums, and the number of zeros (`vegan::nullmodel`, quasiswap), a genus being classed as a host generalist or a host specialist where its breadth lay above the 97.5th or below the 2.5th percentile of the null distribution.

Host generalists and specialists were additionally classified by a two-stage screening of the host species-level presence/absence matrix, applied to ASVs and, for comparison, to genera. In the first stage, a within-column permutation null model that preserves the richness of each host species was used to determine a breadth threshold: for each host, as many taxa as were observed on it were drawn at random over 1,000 iterations to give the null distribution of cross-species prevalence, and the smallest prevalence above the expected breadth at which the observed number of taxa exceeded the null mean was taken as the generalist threshold (six host species for ASVs and seven for genera). In the second stage, to exclude taxa that are broad yet restricted to closely related hosts, the mean pairwise phylogenetic distance (MPD) among the hosts of each taxon was compared, for each prevalence k ≥ 2, with the MPD of every possible set of k host species; with 16 host species all such sets can be enumerated (at most 12,870), so that the null distribution is exact rather than sampled. A standardised z-score was calculated against that distribution, and one-sided p-values for clustering and for dispersion were taken as the proportion of host sets with an MPD no greater, or no smaller, than that observed, each with Benjamini–Hochberg correction. A taxon was classed as a generalist where it met the breadth threshold and was not significantly clustered (q ≥ 0.05), as a specialist where it occurred on a single host species, and as intermediate otherwise. At the ASV level, 29 ASVs were significantly clustered, of which 22 met the breadth threshold and were removed as pseudo-generalists, yielding 241 generalists and 1,120 specialists; no genus was significantly clustered, leaving 53 generalist and 141 specialist genera. A normal approximation to the z-score, which the skewed null distribution does not support, would have classed 195 ASVs and 16 genera as clustered. For each taxon, the representative host genus was recorded as the host genus on which it was detected in the largest share of samples.

To place host generalist and specialist ASVs in an evolutionary framework, separate maximum-likelihood trees were constructed for the two groups (241 and 1,120 sequences). Generalist sequences were aligned with MUSCLE (v5.3), trimmed with trimAl (v1.5; `-automated1`), and the tree was inferred with IQ-TREE (v3.0.1) under ModelFinder model selection (best-fit model TIM3+I+R3 by the Bayesian information criterion) with 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates. Specialist sequences were aligned with MAFFT (v7.526; `--auto`), trimmed with trimAl, and the tree was inferred with FastTree (v2.2.0; `-nt -gtr -gamma`). The generalist sequences all derive from a single region of the 16S rRNA gene (291–293 bp), giving a trimmed alignment of 291 columns without gaps; the ultrafast bootstrap converged after 800 iterations (correlation of split frequencies 0.99), and 49 of 238 internal branches received support of 95% or more, as is to be expected of closely related sequences of this length. The specialist sequences span 252–471 bp, as they derive from studies that sequenced different regions of the gene; the trimmed alignment comprised 466 columns of which 26% were gaps, and the deeper branches of that tree are accordingly uncertain. A third tree, of the ASVs of both classes together (1,361 sequences), was built as for the specialists, in order to show the two classes on a single phylogeny; its trimmed alignment retained the 251 columns shared by all sequences, without gaps. All trees were midpoint-rooted for display, and ASVs of *Akkermansia*, a suspected contaminant that dominates the seeds of a single study, were omitted from the tree figures though retained in every analysis. The combined tree was drawn with ggtree and ggtreeExtra and annotated, from the centre outwards, with host class (also carried by the branches leading only to one class), phylum, the oxygen use predicted by BugBase, one ring per host plant family marking the families on which the ASV was detected, and outer bars giving the number of host species. For the separate generalist and specialist trees, annotation files for iTOL were generated with the `itol.toolkit` R package (v1.1.7): each carries phylum and class colour strips, rings for the BugBase-predicted aerobic type, Gram type, and biofilm formation, and an outer bar giving the number of host species; the specialist tree, in which that number is one throughout, carries the host plant family in addition.

Compositional differences between host generalist and host specialist ASVs were characterised in three ways. Taxon representation was compared at the phylum, class, order, family, and genus ranks with Fisher's exact tests (generalist against specialist ASVs, inside against outside each taxon), with Benjamini–Hochberg correction within rank; taxa occurring exclusively in either group were reported separately. At the genus level, ASVs were aggregated and genera ranked by host breadth, expressed as the number of host species and of host genera on which they were detected, and the 25 most broadly distributed genera were reported together with their mean relative abundance. Finally, within the five phyla richest in ASVs, the relationship between host breadth and non-zero relative abundance (i.e., the mean across the samples in which an ASV was detected) was tested by linear regression, on the abundance itself and on its log10 transform, and by one-sided Spearman correlation, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction across phyla.

All functional traits were inferred from the marker gene by *in silico* prediction and are therefore referred to as predicted traits. ASV × trait tables were compiled from three resources. For BugBase (v0.1.0; Greengenes 13.8), each ASV was matched to its closest Greengenes 97% reference sequence with vsearch (v2.32.0; closed reference, at least 97% identity, both strands) and assigned the precalculated trait values of that OTU, covering aerobic type, Gram type, biofilm formation, stress tolerance, mobile elements, and potential pathogenicity. For FAPROTAX (v1.2.12), ecological functions were assigned from the taxonomy of each ASV; an ASV placed in at least one functional group was treated as annotated, and an ASV placed in none as missing. For metaTraits (GTDB-based genus-level summary), each ASV was assigned the summary of the genus whose name matched its own, the consensus value being used for Boolean traits and the genus median for numeric traits; every ASV of a genus therefore carries the same values. Trait columns were classified as binary or numeric; BugBase columns holding fractions rather than zeros and ones were, accordingly, treated as numeric. Binary traits were analysed among annotated ASVs (missing values excluded) using binomial generalised linear models against log2(n_hosts + 1), with Wald tests and Benjamini–Hochberg correction; traits with fewer than 100 annotated ASVs or with no variation were excluded. Effect sizes were summarised as rank-biserial correlations and as odds ratios per doubling of host breadth with 95% confidence intervals. Numeric traits were tested using Spearman correlations with n_hosts, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Host breadth was capped at 20, although no ASV here exceeds 12 host species, so that the cap was without effect.

To characterise gene-level functional differentiation, the presence or absence of KEGG orthologues (KOs) was predicted for all ASVs with PICRUSt2 (v2.6.2; `--max_nsti 0.15`), a KO being counted as present where its predicted copy number exceeded zero; 2,755 of the 2,823 ASVs had a nearest-sequenced-taxon index within that limit and were retained. KOs present in all or in no ASVs were excluded, leaving 7,883 of 13,793 predicted KOs for association testing. KO occurrence was modelled against log2(n_hosts + 1) in the same logistic framework as the binary traits, with likelihood-ratio tests and Benjamini–Hochberg correction; Wald odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were used to estimate effect sizes. Significant KOs (FDR < 0.05) were classified as generalist-enriched (log2 OR ≥ 0.5) or specialist-enriched (log2 OR ≤ −0.5), with weaker effects left unassigned. KOs were annotated to KEGG level-2 categories (KEGG BRITE ko00001) and to the five-level plant-growth-promoting gene functions of the PGPg_finder database.

To examine the distribution of taxa with distinctive predicted attributes, three special classification groups were constructed from the predicted traits: anaerobic bacteria (BugBase, Anaerobic = TRUE), nitrogen-fixing bacteria (FAPROTAX, nitrogen_fixation = TRUE), and small-genome bacteria (the smallest 1% of non-missing metaTraits genome sizes). For each group, the number of ASVs, the sample-level distribution of relative abundance, the order-level taxonomic composition, and the generalist and specialist composition were quantified, together with host loading, defined as the number of member genera detected per host genus, and its Spearman correlation with the relative abundance of the group. To visualise the distribution of each group, its ASVs were annotated to the finest resolvable taxonomic level and aggregated into a label × host genus matrix of mean relative abundance, retaining labels detected in at least three host genera; the matrix was log10-transformed after adding half of its smallest non-zero value, scaled by row (z-score), and clustered in both dimensions (Ward's method, `ward.D2`); the small-genome group had no label detected in three or more host genera and was not drawn. Differences among host plant orders were tested for each label with Kruskal–Wallis tests, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction.

The headline statistics were checked by an independent audit (`validation/verify_manuscript.py`), in which the filtering cascade, Shannon diversity, Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, PERMANOVA, and PERMDISP are reimplemented from the raw inputs without calling the pipeline's code; the two implementations were kept separate deliberately, so that agreement between them constitutes evidence rather than repetition.

Statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.3.2), with thresholds and grouping variables read from `analysis_config.json`; the predicted-trait tables were compiled in Python (v3.14), and sequence alignment, tree inference, reference matching, and gene-content prediction used the external programs named above. Community analyses (PERMANOVA, Mantel tests, variance partitioning, diversity, CLAM, and null models) used vegan, with phyloseq for data handling and metagenomeSeq for CSS normalisation; phylogenetic signal used phytools and ape, with phangorn for rooting trees; the per-stratum effect sizes and their meta-analysis used metafor; MRM and the two-stage host screening were implemented in R as described above; differential abundance, indicator analysis, and networks used DESeq2, indicspecies, and igraph; and visualisation used ggplot2, ggtree, ggtreeExtra, ggnewscale, ggdendro, and patchwork, with heatmaps drawn in ggplot2 and clustered with `stats::hclust`. The default significance threshold was α = 0.05, and multiple comparisons were corrected with the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate. Permutation-based tests (Mantel, MRM, `adonis2`, `permutest`, `multipatt`, Blomberg's *K*, and the quasiswap null model) were run with 999 iterations, the within-column null model with 1,000 iterations, and the λ bootstrap with B = 500; the MPD null distribution was enumerated in full and involved no random draws. Effect sizes were reported as appropriate (Mantel r, MRM R², marginal R², adjusted R², Spearman ρ, Hedges' g with I², logistic odds ratios, rank-biserial correlation, Blomberg's *K*, and Pagel's λ). The sample size after quality control was 1,886; p-values below 0.001 are reported as p < 0.001.

Several analyses that are coded in the script are not described above. Faith's phylogenetic diversity and UniFrac distances were computed in the last full run but are not used in the analyses reported here; the core microbiome partition, NMDS ordination, and ANCOM-BC2 produced no output in that run.
